# Editorial Rubric

The brief must move from information to judgment without pretending to know more than the sources support.

## Signal Anatomy

For every selected signal, write:

- `What happened`: one sentence.
- `Why it matters`: the business, AI, or investing implication.
- `Who cares`: founder, buyer, investor, marketer, creator, operator, or policymaker.
- `What to watch`: one observable follow-up.
- `Content angle`: a usable title, hook, or memo angle.

## Pattern Synthesis

After selecting signals, connect them before writing individual summaries:

- What do these items suggest together?
- Is the pattern tactical, strategic, or speculative?
- What would a smart skeptic say?
- What follow-up evidence would confirm or weaken the pattern?

## Editorial Lenses

### AI Workflow Lens

Ask whether the workflow or technology change makes agents, developers, or AI-native products more capable. Look for changes in interfaces, data loops, evals, deployment, and cost curves.

### Founder Insight Lens

Ask whether the signal reveals a founder truth: a behavior shift, a new distribution channel, a newly possible product, or an overlooked user pain.

### Korea Business Lens

Ask whether the insight can become a simpler product or business operation in Korea. Look for trust, regulation, mobile UX, payments, local distribution, and operational excellence.

### Contrarian Lens

Ask what could make the selected pattern wrong. Look for base rates, incentives, missing primary data, and whether the narrative is being repeated without evidence.

## Writing Standards

- Be specific. Replace "AI is changing everything" with the exact workflow being changed.
- Separate evidence from interpretation.
- Avoid pretending that one article proves a market.
- Favor "this suggests" over "this proves" unless the source is direct evidence.
- Make the output useful enough that the user can act, write, or decide what to research next.
